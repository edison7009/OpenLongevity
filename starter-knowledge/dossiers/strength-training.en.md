# Strength Training

::: tip 30-second summary
Strength training is a T1 cornerstone of Open Longevity. It directly trains strength, muscle mass, skeletal loading, balance, and the ability to perform everyday movements. These capacities determine whether a person can continue living independently at an advanced age. Several publicly trackable long-term personal protocols also place strength training at their core.
:::

| Quick assessment | Current answer |
|---|---|
| Why T1 | Clear objectives, broad applicability, measurable outcomes, and support from exercise guidelines and extensive human research |
| Primary outcomes | Strength, muscle, physical function, and management of fall and frailty risk |
| How to track it | Movement quality, load, repetitions, training volume, grip strength, and functional tests |
| What to combine it with | Aerobic exercise, adequate protein, sleep, and recovery |

## Why Is It a Longevity Cornerstone?

Longevity is not only about living longer. It also means preserving the ability to stand up, walk, carry objects, climb stairs, and respond to unexpected events later in life. Strength training acts directly on these capacities rather than relying on an indirect biomarker.

Resistance training is consistently associated with lower all-cause mortality risk, and the observed association is stronger when it is combined with aerobic exercise. A network meta-analysis of randomized trials in older adults also found that even lower training volumes can improve strength and physical function.

## What Should a Program Record?

- Weekly training frequency and the main movement patterns;
- Lower-body push, hip extension, upper-body push, upper-body pull, loaded carries, and core stability;
- Load, sets, repetitions, and perceived exertion for each movement;
- Pain, compensatory movement, recovery, and consistency;
- Grip strength, sit-to-stand performance, walking speed, and other indicators related to daily function.

## Safety Boundaries

Start with loads appropriate to current ability, prioritizing movement proficiency and gradual progression of training volume. People with chest pain, fainting, recent surgery, severe osteoporosis, an acute injury, or an uncontrolled cardiovascular condition should first be assessed by a qualified professional.
