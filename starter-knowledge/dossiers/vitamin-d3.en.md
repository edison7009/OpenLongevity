# Vitamin D3

::: tip 30-second summary
Vitamin D is like a “hormone precursor” provided by sunlight, food, and supplements. Its clearest role is to support calcium and phosphorus metabolism and bone mineralization. The VITAL randomized controlled trial of 25,871 people, published in *The New England Journal of Medicine*, found that 2000 IU daily for about five years reduced the relative incidence of confirmed autoimmune disease by 22% (HR 0.61 after excluding the first two years). A meta-analysis published in *BMJ* found that vitamin D3 supplementation reduced all-cause mortality among older adults. **Included in Bryan Johnson’s protocol.**

## In Plain Language: What Does It Do in the Body?

Vitamin D is converted by the liver and kidneys and participates in regulating the absorption and utilization of calcium and phosphorus. It can be understood as a "permission signal" in the bone mineralization system: when there is a severe deficiency, it is difficult for bone raw materials to be used normally even if they exist. However, this system does not push the blood value as high as possible. Excessive intake may cause high calcium and kidney problems.

It is also involved in immune signaling. Experimental and small human studies suggest that active vitamin D affects regulatory T cells (Tregs), pro-inflammatory T cells, and dendritic cells. It is helpful to compare Tregs to "immune brakes", but **changes in Treg numbers or in vitro suppressive function are only mechanisms/surrogates and do not mean that rheumatoid or thyroiditis has been prevented**.

## If I have a family history, can I rely on vitamin D to prevent it?

The VITAL trial provides the most direct randomized evidence to date: **2000 IU daily for 5.3 years produced an HR of 0.78 for confirmed autoimmune disease, a 22% reduction, and an HR of 0.61 after excluding the first two years, a 39% reduction.** Bryan Johnson’s protocol uses a dose of 2000 IU/day.

- VITAL included 25,871 middle-aged and elderly Americans and did not specifically recruit high-risk groups with "family history of rheumatoid or autoimmune thyroiditis and positive antibodies";
- 123 cases were diagnosed with autoimmune diseases in the vitamin D group and 155 cases in the placebo group, HR 0.78 (95% CI 0.61–0.99, P=0.05); based on the random number of people, the rough calculation is about 0.95% vs. 1.20%, with an absolute difference of about 0.25 percentage points;
- After excluding events in the first two years, the HR is 0.61 (95% CI 0.43–0.86), which is often spread as “39% reduction in the next three years”; however, the interaction test of the treatment effect over time is not significant, so the “golden window of immune tolerance” cannot be named based on this;
- The significant subgroup signal for "greater benefit with family history" in the trial was for **Omega-3**, not vitamin D;
- The number of cases of various single diseases is small, and the differences among single diseases such as rheumatoid and thyroiditis have not reached statistical significance.

2000 IU/day is the dose confirmed by VITAL; 4000 IU/day is the tolerable maximum intake listed by the NIH and is not a recommended target. The specific dosage is determined by the doctor based on the underlying risk assessment.

## How Should VITAL and Its Post-Discontinuation Follow-Up Be Read?

| Question | Reliable conclusion | Cannot be deduced |
|---|---|---|
| 5-year period | 2000 IU/day composite outcome HR for diagnosed autoimmune disease 0.78 | Effective for every autoimmune disease |
| “39% in the next three years” | Prespecified analysis excluding the first two years, HR 0.61 | Proof that the effect must increase over time or that a “golden window” exists |
| Two years after discontinuation | Cumulative confirmed cases in 7 years: 255 vs 259, HR 0.98 (0.83–1.17) | The protection disappears immediately after everyone stops taking the drug, or they must take it for life |
| Interpretation of follow-up | The advantages of the randomized intervention were not sustained; 28.3% of participants self-administered more than 800 IU/day during the observation period, diluting differences between groups | Ability to determine optimal course of treatment, rebound with discontinuation, or lifetime dosage |

## Which Foods and Environmental Sources Provide It?

- The skin can synthesize vitamin D under ultraviolet B exposure, but this is affected by season, latitude, skin color, age, sun protection, and lifestyle;
- Salmon, mackerel and other fatty fish and cod liver oil content are high;
- Egg yolks, liver and some mushrooms provide a certain amount;
- Fortified milks, plant-based milks or cereals are often important sources, check local labels.

## How Should Supplements and Labels Be Assessed?

- Labels may indicate micrograms or IU: 1 microgram equals 40 IU;
- Both D3 and D2 can increase 25(OH)D; NIH ODS concludes that D3 usually increases more and lasts longer, but the specific treatment plan depends on the clinical situation;
- 4000 IU/day for the average adult is the tolerable maximum intake listed by the NIH and is not a recommended target;
- Do not base long-term high-dose supplementation on your own based on an online self-test or "best value".

### Is "target greater than 40 ng/mL" true?

Not established as a general purpose. The National Academy of Sciences standards cited in the NIH ODS consider above 20 ng/mL to be adequate for most people and note that above 50 ng/mL may be associated with adverse effects; the 2024 Endocrine Society also did not identify an optimal threshold for disease prevention in generally healthy adults and advises against routine screening. When medical indications such as hypocalcemia, bone disease, malabsorption, etc. are present, testing and targeting should be determined by disease-specific guidelines.

### Does D3 have to be paired with K2?

K2 is involved in blood clotting and bone-related proteins, and the NIH ODS suggests that the role of different forms of vitamin K in arterial calcification and coronary heart disease risk is still being studied. People taking vitamin K antagonists such as warfarin must be evaluated by the clinical team managing anticoagulation before changing their K2 intake.

## Editorial Conclusion

Vitamin D is an essential nutrient, and large randomized trials and meta-analyses support several roles: VITAL found that 2000 IU/day reduced the incidence of confirmed autoimmune disease by 22%; a *BMJ* meta-analysis found lower all-cause mortality among older adults receiving vitamin D3; and correcting deficiency has clear value for bone health. VITAL tested 2000 IU/day. Bryan Johnson’s protocol includes vitamin D.

## Research questions

- Population: generally healthy adults, evaluated separately from people at risk of deficiency or with clear treatment indications;
- Intervention: Vitamin D3, focusing on regular daily doses rather than intermittent pulse doses;
- Control: placebo, recommended dietary intake or clinical standard treatment;
- Primary outcomes: fractures, cancer, cardiovascular events, death, osteomalacia and adverse events;
- Do not consider an increase in serum 25(OH)D per se as a clinical benefit.

## Potential Benefits

- Prevention and treatment of rickets or osteomalacia caused by vitamin D deficiency;
- Some high-risk groups may require dietary fortification, supplementation or medical evaluation;
- In 2024, the Endocrine Society made conditional empirical supplementary recommendations for people over 75 years of age, pregnancy, children and adolescents, etc., but the population and evidence certainty must be documented separately.
- VITAL provides large randomized trial confirmation of 2000 IU/day for the prevention of autoimmune disease, and validation in high-risk populations is advancing.

## Practical Points

- 2000 IU/day is VITAL’s confirmed autoimmune disease prevention dose;
- 4000 IU/day is the NIH tolerable maximum intake and is not a recommended target;
- Above 20 ng/mL is adequate for most people (National Academy of Sciences standards), higher targets are for medical decision-making;
- Whether K2 is suitable for use will be assessed by a doctor based on coagulation status;
- Interpretation of mechanism indicators such as Treg and VDR combined with clinical outcomes.

## Conditions of Use

- First distinguish between "reaching the recommended intake" and "empirical supplementation exceeding the recommended amount";
- When there are medical indications such as malabsorption, hypocalcemia, bone disease, and minimal sun exposure, testing and treatment will be determined by the clinical situation;
- Generally healthy adults should not automatically upgrade their dosage based on “anti-aging” stories alone;
- Laboratory thresholds and optimal target values for vitamin D deficiency are not entirely consistent among all professional organizations.

## Safety Boundaries

- The NIH ODS lists the tolerable maximum intake for adults as 4000 IU per day; this is the upper limit at the population level and the specific therapeutic dose is determined by the physician;
- Overdose can lead to hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, kidney stones or soft tissue calcification, and in extreme cases renal failure and cardiac arrhythmias;
- Combination with calcium will change some risks; situations such as thiazide diuretics, glucocorticoids, orlistat need to be checked;
- Different dosages may be used to treat a clear deficiency and are determined by the physician based on the clinical situation.
- Those with granulomatous diseases such as sarcoidosis, renal insufficiency, parathyroid disease, a history of high calcium or stones, and those currently using thiazide diuretics should not follow the online plan; calcitriol is a prescription drug and cannot replace ordinary D3 on its own;
- Patients on anticoagulant therapy such as warfarin who are considering K2 must first be evaluated by the clinical team managing anticoagulation.

## Evidence Summary

| Record ID | Study type | Population | Period | Main results | Key limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| vd-vital-2019 | Large randomized controlled trial | 25,871 middle-aged and older US adults, not screened for deficiency | Median 5.3 years | D3 2000 IU/day did not reduce invasive cancer or major cardiovascular events | Not equivalent to treatment trial in those with deficiency |
| vd-vital-fracture-2022 | VITAL auxiliary randomized trial | 25,871 people not screened for deficiency, low bone mass or osteoporosis | Median 5.3 years | Total fracture HR 0.98 (95% CI 0.89–1.08) | Applicable to the general population, separate review for patients with established deficiency or osteoporosis |
| vd-vital-bone-2020 | VITAL Bone Health Ancillary Study | Generally healthy middle-aged and elderly subgroup | 2 years | D3 did not improve bone density or major bone structure indicators | Subgroup, limited time |
| vd-es-guideline-2024 | Clinical practice guideline | Disease prevention population | Evidence synthesis | Routine empiric supplementation beyond DRI is not recommended for generally healthy adults aged 19–74 years; routine screening is not recommended for healthy adults | Many are conditional recommendations, and special groups will be discussed separately |
| vd-vital-autoimmune-2022 | VITAL adjuvant randomized trial | 25,871 middle-aged and older Americans | Median 5.3 years | HR for confirmed autoimmune disease 0.78 (0.61–0.99); 123 vs 155 cases | Composite outcome, borderline significant; insufficient single disease; non-family history high-risk trial |
| vd-vital-autoimmune-followup-2024 | Post-randomized trial observational follow-up | 21,592 people continued for 2 years | Total ~7 years | Cumulative HR for the original vitamin D randomized group 0.98 (0.83–1.17); advantage not sustained | Self-supplementation allowed after discontinuation; 28.3% reported use >800 IU/day; this was not continued randomized treatment |
| vd-treg-review-2019 | Systematic review of 8 RCTs | Autoimmune disease/inflammation patients and healthy people | Heterogeneous | Increased Treg proportion or function in some trials | Unable to be pooled; surrogate markers, interpreted in conjunction with clinical outcome trials |
| vd-incident-autoimmune-meta-2025 | Systematic review/meta-analysis of 18 controlled studies | Different supplements and autoimmune outcomes | Heterogeneous | Vitamin D overall RR 0.99 (0.81–1.20) | Wide variation across studies and doses; suggestive of inconsistent conclusions regarding VITAL signal |

## Tier Assessment

- Current Tier: **T2**
