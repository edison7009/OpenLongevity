# Creatine Monohydrate

::: tip 30-second summary
Creatine is like a short-term power bank for muscle: during high-intensity activities such as lifting and sprinting, it helps replenish immediately usable energy more quickly. It is one of the most extensively studied supplements in humans. Hundreds of randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses support improvements in strength, lean mass, and training adaptation. Large cohort analyses published in leading journals including *The Lancet*, such as the PURE study, identify muscle strength and muscle mass as strong predictors of all-cause mortality; preserving muscle is a core pillar of longevity. **If supplementation is being considered, single-ingredient creatine monohydrate is the most extensively studied form.**
:::

## In Plain Language: What Does It Do in the Body?

Muscle contraction requires ATP, but muscle stores very little ATP that is immediately available. The phosphocreatine system acts like a short-term power bank that quickly recharges “spent energy currency” back into ATP. Supplementing creatine increases muscle creatine stores, potentially allowing a little more work within a training set. Over time, what matters is that this extra training volume can help the body develop better strength and muscle adaptations.

It is not a drug that directly “builds muscle.” Muscle growth results from training, protein, and sleep working together; creatine’s value lies in improving training quality. The rationale for cognitive use is that the brain also requires rapid energy buffering. Meta-analyses of randomized trials have identified signals of cognitive improvement, particularly among vegetarians and people who are sleep-deprived.

## Which Foods Provide It?

- Red meat, pork, and fish contain creatine;
- The body also synthesizes it from amino acids;
- Dietary intake is generally lower among vegetarians, but this does not automatically mean supplementation is necessary.

## How Should Supplements and Doses Be Understood?

- Prefer products whose label lists only **Creatine monohydrate**;
- Many studies use a long-term regimen of about 3–5 g/day; this is research context, not an individual prescription for everyone;
- A “loading phase” can raise stores more quickly but is not required to obtain long-term effects;
- A small initial increase in body weight often reflects changes in muscle-cell water content, not an increase in fat.

## Editorial Conclusion

Creatine has some of the strongest evidence among supplements. Hundreds of randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses support its value for strength, lean mass, and training adaptation, and a meta-analysis of randomized cognitive trials also found beneficial effects. Large statistical studies published in leading journals including *The Lancet* show that muscle strength and muscle mass are closely related to all-cause mortality. Maintaining muscle and reducing falls and frailty in older age are the basis of creatine’s relevance to longevity. Bryan Johnson’s current protocol includes it directly.

## Conditions of Use

- Prioritize discussion for people who also perform resistance training;
- Observe strength, training performance, body composition, or specific functional outcomes;
- Do not mistake short-term water gain for fat gain;
- Longevity relevance is mediated through preserving muscle mass and strength and reducing the risks of falls and frailty; large cohort analyses support an inverse association between muscle strength and all-cause mortality.

## Safety Boundaries

There is a relatively extensive body of medium- and long-term safety data in healthy adults. People with kidney disease, during pregnancy, or using medicines that may affect kidney function should be assessed by a physician. Prefer single-ingredient creatine monohydrate with traceable quality.
