# Magnesium

::: tip 30-second summary
Magnesium acts as a “supporting tool” for hundreds of enzymes in the body and participates in energy metabolism, nerve transmission, muscle contraction, and heart rhythm. Large cohort analyses, including NHANES and ARIC, associate magnesium intake inversely with cardiovascular and all-cause mortality; meta-analyses of randomized trials support blood-pressure improvements, especially among people with hypertension or low magnesium status. **Included in Bryan Johnson’s protocol; prioritize legumes, nuts and seeds, whole grains, and leafy greens.**

## In Plain Language: What Does It Do in the Body?

Enzymes are like microscopic machines in the body, and many of them require magnesium ions to work reliably. Magnesium also participates in nerve signaling and muscle contraction and relaxation. Because its functions are so broad, vague symptoms are easily marketed as “magnesium deficiency.” A proper assessment should consider diet, disease, medicines, kidney function, and clinical findings together rather than relying on a single sensation.

## Which Foods Provide It?

- Nuts and seeds such as pumpkin seeds, chia seeds, almonds, and cashews;
- Legumes and soy products;
- Leafy green vegetables such as spinach;
- Whole grains;
- Some mineral waters also provide magnesium.

## How Should Supplements and Labels Be Assessed?

- Read the amount of **elemental magnesium**, not the weight of the entire compound;
- Magnesium citrate, chloride, and some other salts are generally more absorbable than magnesium oxide, although they may also cause diarrhea;
- The choice of salt depends on the goal and tolerance, not only on a ranking of “absorption rates”;
- The adult UL of 350 mg/day applies only to magnesium from supplements and medicines, not magnesium naturally present in food.

## Editorial Conclusion

Magnesium is an essential mineral. Large cohort analyses associate higher intake with lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, while meta-analyses of randomized trials support a blood-pressure-lowering effect, particularly in people with hypertension, those taking antihypertensive medicine, or those with low magnesium. Guidelines also provide some support for migraine prevention. Bryan Johnson’s protocol includes magnesium.

## Research Question

- Population: separate people at risk of deficiency or inadequacy, people with hypertension or migraine, and generally healthy people;
- Intervention: record the dose as elemental magnesium and distinguish the specific salt;
- Comparator: placebo, dietary improvement, or standard treatment;
- Primary outcomes: symptoms, blood pressure, migraine frequency, clinical events, and adverse reactions;
- Do not interpret one normal serum-magnesium result as proof that total stores are necessarily adequate.

## Potential Benefits

- Correcting magnesium inadequacy related to gastrointestinal disease, long-term heavy alcohol use, some medicines, or renal losses;
- A small reduction in blood pressure may occur among people with hypertension or low magnesium;
- Some guidelines support migraine prevention, although commonly used doses may exceed the supplement UL and should be supervised by a healthcare professional.

## Practical Points

- Poor sleep, cramps, and fatigue have many causes; assess magnesium status alongside them;
- Magnesium salts differ in absorption, tolerability, and clinical effects; choose according to the objective;
- Blood-pressure improvement is one pathway to cardiovascular protection and should be evaluated alongside cohort data on mortality outcomes.

## Conditions of Use

- First assess dietary sources: leafy greens, legumes, nuts, seeds, and whole grains;
- Record elemental magnesium, not only the total compound weight;
- Citrate, chloride, lactate, and some other forms are generally more absorbable than magnesium oxide, but the specific choice still depends on the goal and tolerance;
- Kidney impairment, long-term proton-pump inhibitor or diuretic use, or persistent diarrhea requires separate assessment.

## Safety Boundaries

- The adult magnesium UL from NIH ODS is 350 mg/day **from supplements and medicines**; naturally occurring magnesium in food is not included;
- Common adverse effects are diarrhea, nausea, and abdominal cramping;
- Impaired kidney function increases the risk of magnesium toxicity;
- Magnesium can reduce absorption of bisphosphonates and tetracycline or quinolone antibiotics, so doses need to be separated;
- Extremely high doses can cause hypotension, breathing difficulty, arrhythmia, and even cardiac arrest.

## Evidence Summary

| Record ID | Study type | Population | Duration | Main result | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mg-bp-2025 | Systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs | 38 trials, 2709 participants | Median 12 weeks | Mean systolic blood pressure −2.81 and diastolic blood pressure −2.05 mmHg; larger effects among people taking antihypertensive medicines and those with low magnesium | High heterogeneity; not significant in normotensive groups |
| mg-bp-umbrella-2024 | Umbrella review of RCT meta-analyses | 10 reviews, 8610 participants | Varied | Overall blood-pressure effect was small | Overlap between included reviews and heterogeneity in doses and populations |
| mg-ods-2026 | NIH professional fact sheet | Synthesis of nutrition and clinical data | Continuously updated | Defines deficiency risk, formulations, UL, drug interactions, and kidney-function risks | Not an individual clinical trial |
