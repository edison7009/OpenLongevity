# GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

::: tip 30-second summary
GLP-1 is a satiety hormone naturally secreted by the human gut. GLP-1 receptor agonists are prescription medicines that mimic this hormone. They were first used for diabetes and are now widely used for weight management. In July 2026, Bryan Johnson publicly added them to his longevity platform and described them as “potential longevity drugs.” **These are prescription medicines, not supplements, and require medical assessment and a prescription.**
:::

## In Plain Language: What Do They Do in the Body?

After a meal, intestinal L cells release GLP-1, which does three things:

1. **Tells the brain “I’m full”**—it acts on appetite centers in the hypothalamus, reducing the urge to eat and “food noise”;
2. **Slows gastric emptying**—food stays in the stomach longer, prolonging satiety;
3. **Stimulates insulin and suppresses glucagon**—reducing post-meal glucose peaks.

GLP-1 receptor agonists amplify and prolong this natural signal. Endogenous GLP-1 has a half-life of only 1–2 minutes because it is rapidly broken down by DPP-4; modified medicine versions can remain active for days.

::: info Tirzepatide vs Semaglutide
Tirzepatide is dual-targeted: it activates both GLP-1 and GIP receptors. Head-to-head trials in the SURMOUNT program report slightly greater weight loss than semaglutide.
:::

## Why Are Longevity Communities Interested?

### Established Human Benefits

| Endpoint | Evidence |
|---|---|
| 15–25% weight loss over 68–72 weeks | SELECT, STEP, and SURMOUNT randomized trials |
| 20% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) | SELECT trial: semaglutide 2.4 mg in people with overweight and a history of cardiovascular disease |
| Lower HbA1c and improved insulin resistance | Multiple diabetes randomized trials |
| Reduction in systolic blood pressure of ~5 mmHg | Pooled evidence from multiple randomized trials |
| Lower triglycerides, LDL, and inflammatory markers such as CRP | Multiple randomized trials |

### Longevity-Related Areas Under Investigation

- Kidney protection (FLOW: semaglutide slowed progression of diabetic kidney disease);
- Neuroprotection/cognition (under investigation for Alzheimer’s disease);
- Addictive behaviors, including alcohol, nicotine, and compulsive shopping—currently observational signals;
- Anti-inflammatory and immune-modulating effects;
- Direct lifespan extension: supported by animal data; large observational studies report significantly lower all-cause mortality among semaglutide users, while human longevity endpoints are being evaluated in multiple ongoing trials.

## Who Might Consider Them?

::: tip Potential use cases
- BMI ≥ 30 (obesity), or BMI ≥ 27 plus at least one comorbidity such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, or prediabetes;
- Clear signs of metabolic syndrome;
- Severe, persistent “food noise” that cannot be controlled through willpower alone;
- Use under medical supervision with regular follow-up.
:::

::: warning Situations Unsuitable for Self-Directed Use
- Normal BMI and an interest only in “anti-aging”—the risk-benefit balance does not support it;
- Personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma, because animal studies showed a C-cell tumor signal;
- History of pancreatitis;
- Pregnancy, breastfeeding, or planning pregnancy;
- Type 1 diabetes or diabetic ketoacidosis;
- Severe gastrointestinal disease such as gastroparesis.
:::

## Common Adverse Effects

- **Most common:** nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation, often most pronounced during the first 4–8 weeks and then easing;
- Injection-site reactions;
- Rare but serious: pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, and intestinal obstruction;
- Rapid weight loss can include muscle loss, requiring strength training and adequate protein.
