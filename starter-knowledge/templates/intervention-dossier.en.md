## In Plain Language: What Might It Do in the Body?

Use an everyday analogy to explain the mechanism, then immediately state the limits of that analogy. Define each technical term at first use.

## Why Is the Longevity Community Interested?

Keep mechanisms, animal evidence, observational evidence, and human trials separate rather than blending them into one story.

## How Far Has the Scientific Evidence Actually Progressed?

Start with the most important human study that could most change the conclusion, then provide the evidence table. Present key trials fully by evidence level.

For each key outcome, record where possible: applicable population, between-group effect size, uncertainty, duration, evidence strength, and conflicts. If quantitative data are unavailable, explicitly write "not quantified"; do not substitute adjectives for numbers.

## Which Foods Provide It?

List common, practical food sources; explain variability in content and the value of the overall diet. Say so directly if no reliable food sources exist.

## Supplements: Is It Worth Taking, and How Should the Label Be Read?

Address product forms, key label fields, the forms used in the evidence, and whether a confirmed effective dose exists. Do not turn study doses into personal prescriptions.

## Who Needs Extra Caution?

Use `::: warning Safety Boundary` to list common adverse effects, drug interactions, special populations, upper limits or stopping conditions, and product quality.

## Which Factors Might Change the Result?

Discuss baseline status, age, sex, disease, medications, and genetic differences only when supported by sources. When reliable information is unavailable, state that it is unknown; do not infer individual responses from mechanisms.

## Relationships with Medications, Supplements, and Foundational Habits

Check prescription drugs, over-the-counter drugs, other supplements, diet, sleep, and exercise separately. Explain consequences and evidence strength for interactions; do not present theoretical additive effects as proven risks.

## How Can Someone Tell Whether It Works, Does Not Work, or Should Be Stopped?

Describe the targets and time windows used in research, whether baseline or ongoing monitoring is needed, and situations requiring stopping or medical care. State directly when there is no validated personal monitoring protocol.

## Editorial Conclusion

In one paragraph, answer: "For whom, for what goal, to what level of evidence, and what is the most reasonable action now?" A mechanism-only conclusion is prohibited.
