# How to Read Scientific Evidence

::: tip Core principle
Mechanisms can explain why something might work. Human clinical outcomes are needed to determine for whom it actually helps. Use by a public figure or a change in one biomarker cannot establish efficacy on its own.
:::

## Priority Order

1. Clinical guidelines, systematic reviews, and high-quality meta-analyses;
2. Multiple independent randomized controlled trials;
3. A single randomized trial or prospective observational study;
4. Animal, cellular, mechanistic, and expert hypotheses.

Study design does not replace study quality. A weak meta-analysis does not automatically become high-grade evidence.

## Open Longevity Tiers

| Tier | Meaning | Common situation |
|---|---|---|
| T1 | High-priority foundation | Broad applicability with relatively stable human functional or clinical outcomes |
| T2 | Clearer value in defined contexts | Requires a goal, testing, dose boundary, or specific population |
| T3 | Conditional consideration | Short-term markers outweigh long-term functional outcomes |
| T4 | Early human exploration | Small samples, short duration, or combination-product trials predominate |
| T5 | Frontier or highly uncertain | Mechanistic, animal, or case evidence predominates; not a routine option |

A Tier is a research and decision priority. It is not a dose recommendation or a must-buy list.

## Six Questions for Any Claim

1. Were humans, animals, or cells studied?
2. Did a disease or function improve, or only a surrogate marker?
3. Is there a between-group difference or only a within-group change?
4. How large is the effect, including the absolute difference?
5. Does the population match my age, health status, and goal?
6. Are funding, product, and investigator conflicts disclosed?
