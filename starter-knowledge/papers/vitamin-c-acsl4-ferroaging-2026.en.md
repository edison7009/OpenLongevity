# Vitamin C inhibits ACSL4 to alleviate ferro-aging in primates

- Type: basic research plus primate intervention study
- Journal/year: Cell Metabolism, 2026; 38(4):673-693.e17
- DOI: 10.1016/j.cmet.2026.02.010
- PMID/registration number: to be added
- Permanent link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2026.02.010
- Local source file: to be downloaded

## PICO

- Population: middle-aged and older cynomolgus monkeys (roughly equivalent to humans aged 40–50); also includes human senescent cells, tissue validation from older adults, and a high-iron-diet mouse model
- Sample size: number of monkeys not publicly disclosed
- Intervention: vitamin C 30 mg/kg/day, orally in drinking water, for approximately 40 months
- Comparator: age-matched monkeys without vitamin C supplementation
- Duration: approximately 40 months
- Primary endpoint: multi-organ biological age measured by multi-omics aging clocks
- Safety endpoint: no evident adverse events reported

## Results

- Reversal of multi-organ biological age:
  - Brain hippocampal microglia: 7.39 years younger
  - Pancreatic β cells: 6.97 years younger
  - Brown adipose tissue: 4.44 years younger
  - Muscle: 4.41 years younger
  - Skin: 4.17 years younger
  - Kidney: 3.38 years younger
- Ferro-aging markers decreased by 50%–70%
- Brain: reduced neuroinflammation and abnormal protein deposition, with partial improvements in brain structure and connectivity
- Liver: lower triglycerides, higher HDL, and improved glucose metabolism and islet function
- Mouse validation: a high-iron diet (2000 mg Fe/kg) caused cognitive and motor decline and liver injury; liver-targeted CRISPR knockout of ACSL4 produced improvement

## Mechanism: The Ferro-aging Pathway

Iron accumulation → ACSL4 upregulation → lipid peroxidation (increased ROS and malondialdehyde) → increased inflammation and aging markers → multi-tissue functional decline

- ACSL4 is a key lipid-peroxidation-related enzyme. Overexpression can induce senescence in young cells, while knockdown can reduce senescence
- Vitamin C directly binds to and inhibits ACSL4 enzymatic activity (the binding site was confirmed by molecular simulation and point-mutation experiments)
- It also activates the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway, producing dual regulation
- “Ferro-aging” differs from ferroptosis: it is a chronic process and does not directly cause rapid cell death

## Limitations

- The monkey sample size was not disclosed, so statistical power remains to be confirmed
- The 40-month primate experiment is currently the long-duration antioxidant intervention evidence most closely resembling humans
- Biological-age clocks are surrogate endpoints, not hard endpoints such as lifespan or incidence
- Human-equivalent dose extrapolation is uncertain

## Risk of Bias

- Randomization: to be confirmed
- Allocation concealment: to be confirmed
- Blinding: blinding in the animal experiment remains to be confirmed
- Missing data: not reported
- Selective reporting: the multi-organ, multi-omics dataset is extensive, suggesting relatively low risk of selective reporting
- Other: a series of studies from the same team, including a Cell Stem Cell paper on bone-marrow aging

## Relevance to Open Longevity

- Supports: vitamin C was associated with multi-organ biological-age changes in primates; the study proposes mechanisms including ACSL4 inhibition and Nrf2 activation
- Supports: multi-organ biological-age reversal at the primate level provides important evidence for human longevity research
- Applicability boundary: the monkey dose of 30 mg/kg/day cannot be directly converted into a human supplementation recommendation; human dosing and long-term outcomes require clinical validation
