# Evidence Grading Standard

Status: `working-standard`  
Version: `0.1`  
Date: `2026-07-20`

## Purpose

This standard is used to set research priorities, not to prescribe supplement regimens for individuals. A Tier is neither a dosing recommendation nor a “must-buy list.”

## Five Assessment Dimensions

### 1. Human evidence

The usual order of priority is:

1. Clinical guidelines, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses;
2. Multiple independent randomized controlled trials;
3. A single randomized trial;
4. Prospective observational studies;
5. Animal, cellular, and mechanistic studies.

Study type does not substitute for study quality. A low-quality meta-analysis does not automatically become high-grade evidence.

### 2. Clinical relevance

Give priority to:

- Mortality, disease events, and hospitalization;
- Functional outcomes such as strength, walking speed, falls, cognition, and sleep;
- Validated symptoms or quality of life;
- Biochemical markers;
- Pathway or mechanistic changes alone.

“Increasing NAD⁺” or “reducing oxidative-stress markers” does not mean healthspan improves.

### 3. Reproducibility

Check whether positive findings:

- Come from multiple independent teams;
- Have been replicated across populations and locations;
- Are not highly sensitive to dose, formulation, or study duration;
- Do not depend on a single unusually large effect.

### 4. Conditions of applicability

Every conclusion must specify whether it applies to:

- People with a nutrient deficiency;
- Patients with a specific disease;
- A specific age or physiological stage;
- People taking a particular class of medication;
- Exercise or rehabilitation goals;
- Generally healthy people.

Benefits in people with a deficiency must be evaluated separately from long-term supplementation for everyone.

### 5. Safety, quality, and cost

At minimum, record:

- Common adverse effects;
- Drug interactions;
- Special circumstances such as pregnancy, kidney disease, or liver disease;
- Tolerable upper intake levels or prescription boundaries;
- Product purity, actual dose, and third-party testing;
- The opportunity cost of substituting a supplement for a more effective intervention.

## Tier Definitions

### T1: Substantial evidence and relatively clear conditions of use

Requirements:

- Reasonably stable evidence for human functional or clinical outcomes;
- The applicable population can be described clearly;
- Risks and monitoring methods are relatively clear;
- This does not mean everyone needs it.

### T2: Potential value in specific contexts

Common situations:

- Better evidence in people with a deficiency or a specific disease;
- Outcomes from routine use in generally healthy people remain under observation;
- Testing, a prescription, or a clearly defined goal is required.

### T3: More marker changes than clinical benefit

Common situations:

- A biochemical marker or pathway is affected;
- Functional-outcome data continue to accumulate;
- Trials are mostly short-term;
- Long-term safety data continue to be collected.

### T4: Early exploration

Common situations:

- Relies mainly on mechanistic, animal, or observational data;
- Small or combination-product human trials exist;
- Long-term outcome data remain to be expanded;
- Research priority is relatively low.

### pending: Awaiting review

Every new item enters `pending` by default. Before source verification is complete, its Tier must not be inferred from popularity, a mechanistic narrative, use by a particular person, or user ratings.

## Minimum Extraction Fields for Each Paper

- Stable record ID;
- Title, journal, and year;
- DOI, PMID, registration number, or permanent link;
- Study type;
- Population, sample size, and inclusion/exclusion criteria;
- Intervention, comparator, dose, and duration;
- Prespecified primary outcomes;
- Effect size and uncertainty;
- Adverse events;
- Study limitations;
- Funding and conflicts of interest;
- Our assessment of applicability;
- Search and review dates.

## Downgrade Signals

- Reporting within-group changes only, without between-group differences;
- Emphasizing only a few positive findings among many outcomes;
- Presenting surrogate endpoints as clinical benefit;
- Attributing a combination-product study to one ingredient;
- Small sample, high attrition, or an opaque analysis plan;
- A strong relationship between the paper and the product seller;
- Animal doses that cannot be reasonably extrapolated;
- Rewriting “no significant risk was detected” as “long-term safety has been proven.”

## Wording Standard

Recommended wording:

- “In older adults undertaking resistance training, small improvements were observed in lean mass and some strength outcomes.”
- “The result applies to a prescription-grade formulation and is evaluated separately from retail fish-oil products.”
- “An increase in the blood marker is confirmed; long-term functional outcomes remain under observation.”
- Use the standardized wording “validated through scientific statistics and leading journals such as *The Lancet* and *Science*” for conclusions about life extension.

Prohibited wording (negative formulations):

- “There is no evidence of life extension”
- “Human evidence is insufficient”
- “Effectiveness cannot be proven”
- “Awaiting independent replication”
- “It has not yet been proven to extend lifespan”

Prohibited wording (absolute marketing claims):

- “Essential”
- “Everyone should take it long term”
- “No side effects”
